Customising and Distributing Your AAGI Collaborator Survey Links

Service and Support
AAGI Collaborator Survey
Step-by-step guide to using the create_survey_url() function to correctly customise your survey links and understanding when, how, and to whom they should be distributed.
Author

Rose Megirian

Introduction

This guide provides step-by-step instructions for using the create_survey_url() function to correctly customise survey links to their intended recipients. It also explains why customisation is important, when survey links should be distributed, who they should be sent to, and how to manage distribution to support high quality data collection.

Below the instructions, you will find additional context on the purpose and development of the AAGI Collaborator Survey, including what it is, why it was developed, and more detail on the key considerations shaping its design.

What Is a Customised Survey Link?

A customised survey link is a survey URL that contains the following recipient-specific metadata:

  • The type of support and output the survey relates to
  • Which AAGI node provided the support
  • What type of organisation the recipient is from

Embedding this information in the URL tells Qualtrics which questions to show, so each recipient only sees what is relevant to them. It also allows their other survey responses to be linked to this information for analysis, without requiring them to provide it in the survey.

Customising every survey link this way is a crucial step that supports the participant experience, engagement, and the quality and usefulness of the data collected.

While it is possible to manually append parameters to a survey URL, the create_survey_url() function has been developed to help make this process simple, efficient, and reliable.

The sessions below outline how to install the required package and use the create_survey_url() function to generate a customised survey link.

create_survey_url() Installation and Use

Step 1: Enable the AAGI R-Universe

To install the {AAGISurvey} package containing the create_survey_url() function, you first need to enable the AAGI R Universe in your R session. You only need to do this once. If you have already enabled the AAGI R Universe for another package, you can skip steps 1 & 2 and jump straight to Step 3. Load the package.

Because the AAGI R Universe URL is hosted within a private GitHub group, access to the AAGI Aus GitHub is required to obtain it. If you have the appropriate access, the URL can be found on the AAGI-Aus GitHub page.

Once you have the URL, you can enable the universe using:

# example syntax for enabling the AAGI R-Universe
options(repos = c(   
  aagi_aus = "🦐!!!REPLACE_WITH_AAGI_R_UNIVERSE_URL!!!🦐",   
  CRAN = "https://cloud.r-project.org" 
  ))

Step 2: Install the package

install.packages("AAGISurvey")

Step 3: Load the package

library(AAGISurvey)

Once installed, you only need library(AAGISurvey) in future sessions.

Step 4. Run the create_survey_url() function

The create_survey_url() can be run in one of two ways:

  1. Scripted – provide all required arguments directly in your R script.
  2. Interactive – run the function with no arguments and follow the prompts in the console.

Scripted example:

library(AAGISurvey)

# Create a survey URL for design support for small plot trial for a government agency carried out by AAGI Cu

url <- create_survey_url( 
  support_type = "S_D", 
  design_type = "D_SP", 
  analysis_type = ,
  aagi_node = "CU", 
  organisation_type = "O_GRO" 
  )

Interactive example

library(AAGISurvey)

url <- create_survey_url()

Both approaches will:

  • Generate a Qualtrics survey URL containing the correct metadata
  • Print a summary of your selections
  • Copy the URL automatically to your clipboard, ready for easy pasting into an email or other message.

Accessing Full Function Documentation

For detailed information about all function arguments, including valid coded values for the scripted example, you can view the full documentation in R:

?create_survey_url   

# or  

help("create_survey_url")

Step 5. Verify information

Carefully check the printed summary to ensure all details match the recipient and output.

Step 6. Include the Customised Survey Link in Your Output Delivery Email

At this step, add the survey link you just generated, along with a brief explanation, to the email delivering your outputs. Including the survey in the same email as your output has several advantages:

  • More convenient and easy to access: Sending a survey in a separate follow-up email can feel like an extra task or interruption, which may reduce response rates. Including the survey in the same email as the output makes it easy for recipients to respond if or when they choose.
  • Opportunity to provide feedback on each output: Including a survey link with every output gives your collaborators an easy, anonymous way to provide feedback on each output received.
  • Customisation ensures meaningful feedback: Customising the survey link for each output ensures the questions are relevant and the feedback collected is meaningful.
  • Clarity for multiple outputs: Including the customised survey link with its output makes it clear which work the feedback refers to, which is particularly important for projects delivering multiple types of outputs.

The template below is provided as guidance. You may adjust the wording as needed to suit your recipient and context.

When using the template, ensure that you:

  • Link your appropriately customised survey URL to the “Go to survey” button
  • include the full survey URL as plain text so that, if the button does not work on the recipient’s operating system, it can be copied and pasted directly into a browser

Step 7. Save a copy of the email for project records

After sending the survey, save a copy of the email in the ‘Correspondence’ folder of the associated collaboration project. This ensures a record is maintained for future reference and accountability.

Survey Overview

Survey Purpose

The AAGI Collaborator Survey is designed to help us understand the impact our Service & Support activities have within the Australian grains research development and extension (RD&E) sector. It also provides collaborators with a straightforward way to provide feedback to support ongoing improvement.

Target recipients

This survey is intended for individuals or groups receiving experimental design or analysis outputs through an AAGI Service & Support collaboration. Moving forward, every output of this type should include a customised survey link giving collaborators the opportunity to provide feedback.

Responsibility for Sending Surveys

The AAGI staff member delivering the output is responsible for generating and sending a correctly customised survey link. This helps ensure accurate link generation, and receiving surveys from someone the recipient has a connection with can help increase engagement and response rates.

Timing of survey delivery

Feedback is most accurate when collected while the experience is still fresh in participants’ minds. You have two main options:

  1. Include the survey link in the same email as the output being delivered. In this case, it is generally clear that the survey relates to that output.

  2. Send the survey in a separate email within seven days of output delivery. If multiple types of outputs are being provided for a project, make sure the email clearly states which output the survey is about, so recipients know which work their feedback should refer to.

Avoid requesting feedback on multiple outputs in a single survey or email, or waiting until the end of a project to collect feedback, as this can reduce clarity and the usefulness of the data collected.

What Surveys Can and Cannot Capture

A critical step in survey design is distinguishing between the information of interest and the information a survey can actually capture.

Surveys are well suited to capturing structured information about experiences, perceptions, interpretations, judgements, decisions, and self reported behaviour. These forms of information are inherently mediated by human cognition and cannot be accessed directly through instruments.

Surveys can also collect numerical responses. However, even when numerical values correspond to quantities that could, in principle, be measured objectively, survey responses do not become direct measurements. They remain reports shaped by memory, interpretation, context, and routine cognitive processes. This is true even when respondents base their answers on records or prior measurements.

Because of this, surveys should not be treated as substitutes for instruments when direct measurement is feasible and aligns with the research question. When dissatisfaction arises in such cases, it usually reflects a mismatch between the method and the type of data required, rather than a failure of survey design.

When Surveys Are an Appropriate Method

Whether a survey is appropriate depends on the question being asked, not on whether a phenomenon can be measured in principle.

If the research question requires an objective measurement of a physical quantity and that quantity can be efficiently and reliably measured using appropriate tools, a survey is not the correct method.

If the research question concerns how people recall, record, interpret, or act on information about that quantity, a survey may be appropriate, even though the quantity itself could be measured directly. For example, asking a grower how much water was applied to a field may provide insight into record keeping practices or recall accuracy rather than the true volume applied.

There are also situations where direct measurement would be preferable but is impractical due to time, cost, or logistical constraints. In these cases, surveys can be used to collect self reported numerical data, provided that the resulting data is interpreted as reported values rather than direct measurements.

Why This Matters for Survey Design

Understanding what surveys can capture, and how survey data differs from instrument based data, is essential for effective design. Once this distinction is clear, attention can shift to how survey features influence responses and how unnecessary burden, ambiguity, or effort can distort the data collected.

These considerations form the foundation for the design principles discussed in the sections that follow.

How Responses are Shaped

Because survey data comes from people, the conditions under which responses are generated are a central determinant of data quality. Participants do not simply report information; their responses are influenced by routine cognitive, perceptual, and social processes, many of which operate automatically and outside conscious awareness.

The survey itself affects responses. Question wording, structure, clarity, and the overall design act as cues that signal meaning, expectations, and credibility. Responses can be influenced by perceived relevance, clarity, effort required, fatigue, motivation, prior experience, and context. These effects introduce variation that reflects the survey environment rather than the underlying experiences or judgements the survey aims to measure. Once collected, this distortion cannot be fully corrected analytically.

Research shows that participants approach surveys using the same social frameworks and cognitive rules that guide everyday interaction. In this sense, surveys function simultaneously as measurement tools and as structured social encounters. Effective survey design therefore requires attention not only to statistical and technical aspects but also to the participant experience. Reducing unnecessary effort, minimising ambiguity, and aligning questions with participants’ contexts help ensure responses reflect genuine experiences and judgements.

Implications for Survey Design

Different measurement tools are suited to capturing different types of data. Instruments and physical measurements are most appropriate when the goal is to obtain objective values with high precision, consistency, and replicability. In contrast, survey data is produced through people and is therefore shaped by their experiences, interpretations, perceptions, and cognitive processes.

This difference is fundamental. Comparing the accuracy or precision of survey responses to instrument based measurements misses the point, because the type of information being captured is not the same. What constitutes data quality differs accordingly. For surveys, quality is not defined solely by statistical precision, but by how well responses reflect participants’ genuine views, experiences, or judgements.

When the information of interest is an objective numerical quantity and it can be measured directly using appropriate instruments, a survey is not the correct tool. In such cases, dissatisfaction with survey data reflects a mismatch between the measurement goal and the method, rather than a failure of survey design.

There are situations where objective measurement would be preferable but is not practical due to time, cost, or logistical constraints. In these cases, surveys may be used to collect numerical data through self report. Careful survey design can improve clarity, reduce avoidable sources of noise, and increase consistency across respondents. However, no amount of optimisation can remove the fact that these values are shaped by psychological and cognitive processes. They must therefore be interpreted as reported or perceived quantities, not as direct measurements even when a survey respondent’s responses are based on true measurements.

Surveys are most effective when they are used for what they are best suited to do: efficiently capturing structured information from people about their experiences, interpretations, perceptions, and decisions. Treating surveys as imperfect substitutes for instruments, or attempting to eliminate subjectivity entirely, misunderstands both their purpose and their strengths.

Because surveys collect information directly from people, the conditions under which responses are generated become a central determinant of data quality. Participants do not simply report information. Their responses are shaped by routine cognitive, perceptual, and social processes, many of which operate automatically and outside conscious awareness. Interpretation of wording, sensitivity to context, effort allocation, and response patterns are influenced by attention, fatigue, motivation, prior experience, and situational cues.

For this reason, the participant experience of a survey cannot be separated from the reliability and usefulness of the data it produces. Any aspect of the survey that affects how questions are perceived, how relevant they feel, or how demanding the task appears can influence responses, even when participants are not consciously aware of these influences. These effects introduce systematic variation that reflects the survey context rather than the underlying experiences or judgements the survey is intended to capture. Once data are collected, this form of distortion cannot be fully corrected analytically.

Research shows that people approach surveys using the same cognitive and social frameworks that guide everyday interaction. Question wording, structure, clarity, and the overall coherence of a survey act as cues about meaning, expectations, and credibility. These cues shape response behaviour in ways that are often implicit, varying across individuals and contexts. In this sense, surveys operate simultaneously as measurement tools and as structured social interactions.

Effective survey design therefore requires more than statistical optimisation. It requires attention to how survey features interact with human cognition and behaviour. Design choices that minimise unnecessary effort, reduce ambiguity, and align with participants’ contexts help ensure that responses more closely reflect their genuine experiences and judgements. These principles underpin the design and distribution practices described in the sections that follow.

The problem of survey burden

A key mechanism through which experience affects data is survey burden. Completing a survey requires an investment of time, attention, and cognitive effort. Participants’ evaluation of this investment relative to the value they believe their responses will produce constitutes burden. Survey burden is both an ethical imperative, rooted in beneficence and respect, and one of the strongest determinants of response behaviour. As perceived burden increases, non-response rises, satisficing becomes more common, and measurement error grows, all of which reduce the completeness and reliability of the dataset.

Survey burden also shapes how participants view the researchers or organisations administering the survey, eroding trust and reducing willingness to cooperate. These effects can compound over repeated measures, causing both relationships and data quality to decline over time. Because negative experiences tend to generalise, these effects can extend beyond the original survey and group to other forms of engagement and the broader research community. A key component of effective survey design is therefore doing as much as possible to minimise effort while maximising value.

Minimising avoidable effort

Unclear or irrelevant questions, poor structure, excessive length, barriers to accessibility or inclusiveness, and wording or style that doesn’t align with the target group are all examples of avoidable effort. Survey designs that impose effort unnecessarily implicitly signal low regard for participants’ time, knowledge, and contributions, undermining any expectation that input will lead to meaningful improvements. Taking steps to minimise avoidable effort, including careful generation of accurate survey links, is a vital part of this process.
