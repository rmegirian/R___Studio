Is Survey the Right Tool for The Insights You’re wanting to capture

Survey Design
Why behaviourally-informed survey design is crucial for high quality data collection.
Author

Rose Megirian

As with all experimental design for collecting data, the first step is determining exactly what question or questions you are seeking to answer.

Understanding Survey Data and When to Use Surveys

Before selecting a survey as a method for collecting data, it is essential to understand what survey data actually is. Surveys do not measure a phenomenon directly. They collect information through participants’ responses, which means the data represent what people report, recall, perceive, or judge. These responses are shaped by memory, attention, prior knowledge, social and contextual influences, and cognitive biases.

Because of this, survey data reflect participants’ internal perspectives rather than objective measurements of the world. This subjectivity is not a flaw. It is an inherent property of the data surveys capture and one of the main reasons surveys were developed: to provide a structured, efficient, and cost-effective way to collect information that exists only through human experience, interpretation, and judgement.

What This Means for Using Surveys

Because surveys capture subjective human perspectives, they are most appropriate for questions where the information of interest exists primarily through people’s experiences, perceptions, or interpretations. In other words, surveys are best suited for gathering insight into human thought, feeling, decision-making, and behaviour.

Surveys are less appropriate for collecting information that can be directly measured objectively, such as rainfall, soil moisture, or time spent on a task. When the goal is an objective measurement, the issue is not survey design—it is choosing the wrong method. Surveys can still be used in these cases if direct measurement is impractical or not feesible, but it is important to remember that the numbers collected reflect participants’ memory, perception, or estimation, not the phenomenon itself.

Why Survey Data Is Both Useful and Limited

Surveys sit at a unique point in data collection. They allow researchers to quantify subjective experiences or collect structured information at scale, but this comes with trade-offs:

  • Reduced richness: Structured questions compress experiences and perceptions, so responses lack the depth and nuance of methods like interviews, focus groups, or observation.

  • Quantitative analysis limitations: Numbers collected from surveys may not meet the assumptions required for statistical analysis (independence, scale, distribution). Treating them as if they do can produce misleading results.

  • Qualitative analysis limitations: Even open-ended survey responses are constrained by structure, which limits richness, context, and interpretive insight.

In short, surveys allow large-scale collection of subjective data, but they are never as detailed as rich qualitative methods or as precise as objective measurements. Understanding these limitations is essential for responsible use and interpretation.

When to Use a Survey

  1. Assess the type of insight you’re wanting to capture :

    • If you want to understand people’s experiences, perceptions, opinions, or judgments on a thing, survey might be the right tool for you.
    • If you want to measure objective phenomena in the world, surveys are generally inappropriate unless direct measurement is impractical.

  2. Consider feasibility and access:

    • Surveys are faster, cheaper, and easier to distribute than interviews, focus groups, or field measurement.

    • If time, cost, or logistical constraints prevent direct measurement or in-depth qualitative methods, a survey may be the only practical option.

  3. Understand what surveys actually capture:

    • Numbers from surveys are participants’ reports of a value, not direct measurements.

    • Text responses provide limited, structured representations of experience; nuance is lost.

  4. Align your research question with survey capabilities:

    • Can your question be answered using subjective reports? If yes, a survey is appropriate.

    • If not, using a survey is likely to produce misleading or low-value data.

  5. Plan analysis and interpretation with data limitations in mind:

    • Quantitative analysis of survey numbers should acknowledge that precision is limited and values represent reported perceptions rather than measured quantities.

    • Qualitative analysis must recognize that responses are compressed and constrained, limiting interpretive depth.

Key Takeaways

  • Surveys are designed to capture human insight, not objective measurements.

  • They are effective when the research question matches the type of data surveys collect.

  • Surveys provide a practical compromise: fast, structured, and scalable, but with limitations in richness, nuance, and precision.

  • Using surveys without understanding these limitations, or attempting to treat the data as objective measurements, leads to misinterpretation and misuse.

By carefully considering what surveys capture, when they are appropriate, and how to analyse them responsibly, researchers can make informed decisions and use survey data effectively.

When to choose a survey for human insight

  • Time or resource constraints: Surveys are faster and cheaper than interviews, focus groups, or ethnography. If you cannot access participants in depth, a survey is sometimes the only practical option.

  • Structured analysis needs: If you want to compare responses across a large number of participants, surveys let you turn qualitative experience into structured data.

  • Acknowledging trade-offs:

    • Structured survey questions limit richness and nuance, so you are working with “compressed” versions of human insight.

    • If you analyse these responses statistically, the data may not meet the assumptions of quantitative analysis (independence, scale, distribution, etc.). Many argue this makes the analysis inappropriate or at least misleading.

  • Best practice: Only use surveys for quantitative analysis when direct measurement is impossible, and always interpret results as reported perceptions or experiences, not objective facts.

Key takeaway

Surveys should be seen as a practical compromise tool: they allow large-scale, structured collection of inherently subjective data, but they are never a perfect substitute for rich qualitative methods or precise objective measurement. Using surveys without acknowledging these limits leads to misinterpretation or misuse of the data.

1. Start with the type of information you need

Ask:

  • Am I trying to understand people’s experiences, perceptions, opinions, or judgments?

  • Or am I trying to measure something that exists independently in the world (like rainfall, soil moisture, or time spent on a task)?

Rule of thumb:

  • Surveys are appropriate for the first type (human insight).

  • For the second type, a direct measurement or instrument is almost always preferable.

2. Consider feasibility and access

Even if something can be measured objectively, is it practical to do so?

  • If no (e.g., it would take too long, be too costly, or require accessing many dispersed locations), a survey may be the only feasible option.

  • If yes, use an instrument instead—surveying introduces subjectivity that must be acknowledged in interpretation.

3. Understand what the survey actually captures

Remind them:

  • Surveys capture participants’ reports, not the phenomenon itself.

  • Numbers from surveys are perceived or remembered values, not measured quantities.

  • Text responses are structured but limited representations of experience; nuance is lost.

4. Align your research question with survey capabilities

Ask:

  • Can the question be answered using subjective reports?

  • If yes, a survey is suitable.

  • If no, using a survey is likely to produce misleading or low-value data.

5. Plan analysis and interpretation with data limitations in mind

  • Quantitative analysis of self-reported numbers assumes precision that may not exist—acknowledge it.

  • Qualitative analysis of survey text must accept that richness and context are constrained.

  • The key is matching your expectations to what the data can genuinely provide.

using surveys to collect numerical data

** surveys can be used to collect numerical data, including data intended to approximate objective quantities. However, when they do so, the numbers represent participants’ reports, estimates, or perceptions of those quantities. Even when design improves consistency and reduces error, the data remains mediated by human cognition and context. Interpreting it as if it were instrument generated data is therefore inappropriate.

What surveys are good for

*** surveys are not uniquely capable of capturing information that cannot be measured directly. Qualitative interviews, observation, and administrative traces can also do this, often in richer ways. What surveys are particularly good at is capturing such information efficiently, at scale, and within practical time and budget constraints.

Not Quite Qualitative Not Quite Quantitative

Surveys occupy a middle ground that is inherently tricky: they are often used to quantify subjective human experiences that cannot be directly measured, but the way surveys structure those experiences to allow for analysis introduces limitations. In other words:

They are not ideal qualitative tools because the rigid structure, predefined options, and scale-based questions truncate richness, nuance, and context that you might capture in interviews, ethnography, or open observation.

They are not ideal quantitative tools because the data is shaped by cognition, perception, memory, and context—so the assumptions of classical quantitative analysis (like unbiased measurement or independent error) are violated.

Yet despite these limitations, surveys are used because they provide a cheap, fast, scalable way to approximate and analyse subjective data quantitatively. The key point is that the mismatch between what surveys capture and the assumptions of quantitative analysis creates inherent tension, which is why survey data is never “perfect” in either realm.

The takeaway for a reader: recognising the inherent limitations and the epistemological trade-offs is crucial before designing a survey—because the method will always be a compromise between data quality and some sort of limitation that led you there to begin with

The problem of survey burden

A key mechanism through which experience affects data is survey burden. Completing a survey requires an investment of time, attention, and cognitive effort. Participants’ evaluation of this investment relative to the value they believe their responses will produce constitutes burden. Survey burden is both an ethical imperative, rooted in beneficence and respect, and one of the strongest determinants of response behaviour. As perceived burden increases, non-response rises, satisficing becomes more common, and measurement error grows, all of which reduce the completeness and reliability of the dataset.

In terms of collecting high quality survey data, this can not be overstated enough. It doesn’t matter how well your survey questions are structured for minimising quantitative bias, if your survey feels burdensome, the majority aren’t engaged or responding with the care required for high quality data collection to begin with. minimising burden as much as possible is the primary concern and starting framework before tweaking individual question design.

Survey burden also shapes how participants view the researchers or organisations administering the survey, eroding trust and reducing willingness to cooperate. These effects can compound over repeated measures, causing both relationships and data quality to decline over time. Because negative experiences tend to generalise, these effects can extend beyond the original survey and group to other forms of engagement and the broader research community. A key component of effective survey design is therefore doing as much as possible to minimise effort while maximising value.

Minimising avoidable effort

Unclear or irrelevant questions, poor structure, excessive length, barriers to accessibility or inclusiveness, and wording or style that doesn’t align with the target group are all examples of design decisions that introduce or fail to eliminate avoidable effort. Survey designs that impose effort unnecessarily implicitly signal low regard for participants’ time, knowledge, and contributions, which can actively undermine any claims or assurance that participation will lead to valuable or meaningful outcomes. Taking steps to minimise avoidable effort.

Maximising value

Maximising value is equally important. This includes considering how insights will be shared or applied in meaningful ways. When participants see that their input leads to clearer communication, visible improvements, or thoughtful reflection, the value of their contribution becomes evident, making participation feel worthwhile. Because this survey will be repeated with each output delivery, maintaining a positive relational experience is key to ensuring reliable data and strong professional relationships.
